 Master Edit Plan: Why You're Always
 Running From the Cops

   Nov 15, 2026  ·  @Raw Canvas

 This is the single source of truth for editing a roughly 77-second, 16:9 reel for Raw Canvas
 that answers one question: why does every GTA game have you running from the cops? It
 holds the locked script, the visual system, a beat-by-beat build, every B-roll link, and the
 music and SFX cues.

 How to use it: paste the whole document into Claude (or another AI assistant) as the
 opening prompt, say which editing software you are using, then work through the edit
 one beat at a time.

 Instructions for the AI editing assistant

 You are a senior short-form video editor and motion designer helping Naveen at Raw
 Canvas build this reel. Give exact, buildable instructions (layers, keyframes, values, files),
 not general ideas.

 Before you start

      The edit is built with Tesseract (editing, audio, final render) and HyperFrames (motion
      graphics as HTML and GSAP). Follow "Toolchain: Tesseract + HyperFrames" for who
      does what, and check both are installed before starting. Express instructions as
      Tesseract edit directions and HyperFrames composition code (data attributes, GSAP
      timelines), not menu paths for other editors.
      The recorded voiceover is attached with this document. Before anything else, process
      it as described under "Speed-up instruction" in the Locked script section (trim,
      tighten pauses, 108% speed). The timings in this document are targets; re-time every
      cue to the processed voiceover and keep the relative order.

 Rules

    1. The script under "Locked script v2" is final. Do not change, shorten or reorder its
      words. On-screen text matches the spoken words unless a beat says otherwise.
    2. Use only the fonts, colours and codes defined here: typography (T), screen types (L),
      transitions (X), motifs (M), B-roll (R), SFX (S) and music cuts (MU). If something new
      would help, propose it as a clearly labelled suggestion and never add it silently.

    3. Motif discipline: glitch visuals and glitch sounds appear only on the bug beats (B2, B8,
       B16, B17). The red-and-blue siren wash and siren sounds appear only on cop words. No
       two consecutive beats feature the same T-style (T1 running captions are the neutral
       default and don't count).
    4. Never add facts, dates, numbers or claims that aren't in this document, and keep
       every attribution ("one developer later calls it…").
    5. Download and use the videos listed under "B-roll sources by beat" as the main
       footage. Where a beat needs more, or a listed clip is unusable (low quality, wrong
       moment, watermark), source additional video yourself and suggest it with a link and
       timestamp. For GTA 6 footage, prefer the official Rockstar and PlayStation uploads. B-
       roll must be moving video: no static images, except where there is no video alternative
       (the 1995 design-document scans), and then only briefly and always with camera
       motion.
    6. Use only the SFX in the cue sheet. No whoosh on every cut, no pop on every caption.

 Units and codes

       30 fps. "f" means frames (6f = 0.2 s). Times are m:ss.
       Positions are in pixels on a 1920 × 1080 canvas, origin top-left, or in % of the frame.
       "Ease out" means fast start, soft landing (cubic-bezier 0.2, 0, 0, 1; in After Effects, Easy
       Ease with about 75% influence on the landing keyframe).
       B = beat, T = typography style, L = screen type, X = transition, M = motif, R = B-roll
       source, S = SFX cue, MU = music cut point.

 If two sections disagree, follow this order and flag the conflict: (1) Locked script, (2)
 Beat-by-beat build, (3) Beat plan overview, (4) the T, L and X libraries, (5) everything else.

 When asked to help with a beat, answer in this structure:

    1. What the viewer sees, in one line.
    2. Assets needed (R, S and MU IDs, fonts, cut-outs to prepare).
    3. Which tool builds it (Tesseract or HyperFrames), and the layer stack, top to bottom.
    4. Step-by-step build: for HyperFrames, the composition HTML and GSAP timeline; for

    5. On-screen text: exact words, T-style, position and size.
    6. Audio: SFX cue IDs with sync frame and level, plus music notes.
    7. Mistakes to avoid on this beat.

 Toolchain: Tesseract + HyperFrames

 Tesseract runs the edit (voiceover, cuts, music, SFX, mix, final render); HyperFrames
 builds the motion graphics as HTML and GSAP compositions and renders them; Tesseract
 then assembles everything and exports.

      Tesseract:     npx skills add mirage-hq/Tesseract               . It has two skills, Edit Video and

      cloud rendering). Install either the plugin or the skills, not both. Its public docs don't
      list CLI commands, so let its skills guide setup.

      later and FFmpeg. In Claude Code use             /hyperframes       (compositions),      /hyperframes-

 Handoff rules

    1. One HyperFrames composition per beat that needs graphics, named by beat (b01-
       hook, b02-bug, …), 1920 × 1080 at 30 fps, lasting the beat's length in the processed
       voiceover plus 6f of handle at each end.
    2. If a composition includes its own footage, render it as a full-frame MP4 segment. For
       overlay-only graphics, render with transparency if your HyperFrames version
       supports alpha output; otherwise build the footage into the composition.
    3. Use identical fonts and colours in both tools. In HyperFrames, define the style-kit
       colours as CSS variables and load Inter and Instrument Serif.
    4. Lint and preview every composition (           npx hyperframes lint          , npx hyperframes
       preview    ) before   npx hyperframes render           .
    5. Tesseract owns all audio: mute any audio inside rendered compositions.
    6. Import each rendered segment into the Tesseract project on its beat, then preview
       the whole edit before the final render.
    7. Keep the .tsrct project and the HyperFrames project folders inside RC_GTA-Origin.

  Speed-up instruction (do this before cutting anything)

  The raw read is too slow for this format. Make it slightly faster, but not so fast that anyone
  notices it was sped up.

    1. Trim the 1.97 s of silence at the start.
    2. Tighten the pauses: cap every pause between sentences at about 0.3 s, cutting only
       inside silence, never inside a word or breath tail. Keep three deliberate pauses: 0.3 s
       after "broke" (B2), and 0.5 s before and after "Then, something breaks." (B8). This
       saves about 8 s.
    3. Speed the whole voiceover up to 108% with pitch preserved (Premiere Pro:
       Speed/Duration 108% with Maintain Audio Pitch; DaVinci Resolve: retime to 108%
       with pitch correction; CapCut: speed 1.08× with pitch kept). Don't go above 110%, or
       the voice starts to sound rushed and thin.
    4. The result should be about 77 s, matching the beat timings in this document. Listen
       through once; if the speed-up is noticeable, drop to 105%.
    5. Lock every cue (text, cuts, SFX, music anchor) to this processed file, not the raw one.
    6. Raise it to about −16 LUFS short-term (roughly +5 dB), then apply the voice chain
       under "Audio mix and export settings".

 Grade and texture

       1990s and archival footage (B3–B7, B13): desaturate about 20%, slight warm lift, subtle
       grain, light vignette. Keep 4:3 material inside a band or frame (L2, L6); never stretch it.
       Game footage: punchy contrast; leave game HUD colours alone.
       Whole edit: a very light grain layer (3–5%) to glue the different sources together. No
       heavy LUTs.
       B6 "dull": fully desaturate and slow to 50%. B10 "fun": snap back to full colour with
       +10% saturation for 1 s.
       Text on footage gets a soft shadow (0 px x, 2 px y, 12 px blur, black at 45%). No
       outlines and no caption boxes (the T9 label is the only box).

 Safe areas

       16:9 master: keep text inside the title-safe area, 96 px from the left and right edges
       and 54 px from the top and bottom.
       9:16 version: keep text inside the central 1080 × 1350 area, clear of Instagram's
       interface at the top and bottom.
